Student budget abroad: what it really costs

By StudyWork Editorial Team· Reviewed September 2026· 7 min read

A study-abroad budget has three layers: one-time costs, tuition and monthly living costs. Most students underestimate the first and the third. Here's how to build a budget that survives contact with reality.

1. One-time costs

  • Application fees, English tests and document translation
  • Visa fee and any immigration health surcharge or insurance
  • Flights and initial travel
  • Rental deposit (often 1–2 months' rent) and basic furniture
  • Tuition deposit

2. Monthly living costs

Rent is usually 40–60% of a student's monthly spend, and it varies hugely by city. A shared room in a smaller city can cost half of a studio in a capital. Add food, transport, phone, utilities, study materials and a buffer of 10–15%.

3. Proof of funds

Most student visas require evidence that you can pay tuition and living costs without relying on part-time work. Governments publish minimum amounts that are updated periodically — for example Canada's cost-of-living requirement, the UK's monthly maintenance amounts (higher for London), Australia's annual living-cost figure and Germany's blocked-account amount. Check the current figure on the official site before you apply.

4. How much can part-time work cover?

At term-time limits of roughly 20–25 hours a week, earnings often cover a substantial share of living costs in high-minimum-wage countries — but rarely tuition. Holiday work helps smooth the year. Model it in the earnings estimator, then check your total picture in the funding-gap calculator.

5. Money-saving moves that actually work

  1. Choose housing within one transit zone of campus.
  2. Cook in batches; eat on campus only when it's subsidised.
  3. Use student discounts and student transit passes.
  4. Move money with low-fee transfer services, not airport kiosks.
  5. Buy used textbooks or use the library.
